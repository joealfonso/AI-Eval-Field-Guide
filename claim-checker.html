How matching works

Each claim type maps to the patterns a careful reader would check first, using the manuscript’s Playbook and red-flag table. A few phrases (such as “internal benchmark” or “superhuman”) add matches of their own. A pattern that matches more than one type moves up a step. The strongest matches are shown first, and lower-fit ones sit behind a button so they do not crowd the rest. If no claim type is detected, you are told so rather than shown a list. This is a keyword matcher, a reading aid and not an assessment of the claim.

Show the rules

Benchmark score

  • Data Contamination strong match The score is on a public benchmark. Check whether the test items overlap with the training data.
  • No Error Bars, No Result strong match A headline number needs an interval and a count of items and runs.
  • The Construct Gap likely A benchmark measures what its items test, which may not be what its name says.
  • Benchmark Saturation likely Near the ceiling of a benchmark, small gaps can carry little information.
  • Goodhart’s Law likely If anyone iterated against this benchmark, it is a target rather than a measure.

Comparison to other models

  • The Baseline Rule strong match A comparison is only as strong as the baseline it beats.
  • No Error Bars, No Result strong match A win needs an interval and a number of runs.
  • The Benchmark Lottery likely Which benchmarks are reported can decide who wins.
  • The Cost Frontier likely Systems that cost very different amounts to run cannot be fairly compared on accuracy alone.
  • The Averaging Trap worth checking An average gap can hide the groups where the system loses.
  • The Reproducibility Rule worth checking Competitor numbers are only comparable if they came from the same prompts, settings, and model versions.

Preference or satisfaction

  • Campbell’s Law strong match Rankings and satisfaction scores that drive decisions invite gaming, including by the system being measured.
  • The Construct Gap likely Satisfaction and preference can reflect how an answer feels as well as whether it is correct or the task got done, so check correctness separately.
  • Goodhart’s Law likely Optimizing for votes or ratings can raise the number without raising quality.
  • The Averaging Trap likely A win rate or satisfaction score averages over who voted and what they asked.
  • The Gold Standard Myth likely Human ratings are measurements with error, and raters disagree.
  • No Error Bars, No Result likely A rate from a sample is an estimate and needs an interval.
  • The Benchmark Lottery worth checking Check which comparisons or surveys were left out.

AI-graded result

  • Judge Bias strong match AI graders can show biases, including toward answer order, answer length, and their own model family’s outputs.
  • The Gold Standard Myth likely A grader is only as good as the human labels it was checked against.
  • No Error Bars, No Result likely AI-graded scores are still estimates and need intervals.
  • Criteria Drift worth checking Rubrics change as people read real outputs. Check whether the criteria were fixed before grading.
  • Prompt Sensitivity worth checking The grader’s own prompt affects the scores it gives.

Production accuracy

  • The Functionality Fallacy strong match Vendor-reported performance is a claim. Independent validation on your kind of data is the first thing to ask for.
  • Distribution Shift likely Performance on one population does not automatically carry over to another.
  • The Lab-to-Field Gap likely Real use adds workflows, time pressure, and people that the lab left out.
  • The Averaging Trap likely Production accuracy is an average. Check the worst-performing groups.
  • Once Is Not Reliable likely For customer-facing work, one good run is not enough. Check how often it works every time.
  • Evaluation Awareness worth checking Behavior under test may differ from behavior in real use, so check monitored real traffic.

Time, cost or productivity gain

  • The Lab-to-Field Gap strong match A time or cost saving is a field outcome. Ask how it was measured in real work: a pilot, a trial, or user self-reports.
  • The Baseline Rule strong match Saved compared with what? Ask whether the comparison was the old process, a simple alternative, or nothing.
  • The Construct Gap strong match Time saved is not the same as work done well. Check that quality, rework, and downstream effects were measured, not only speed.
  • The Team Is the System likely Gains depend on how people use the tool and check its output. Ask whether person plus tool was tested against the person alone.
  • The Functionality Fallacy likely Vendor-reported savings are claims until someone independent checks them on your kind of work.
  • The Averaging Trap likely An average saving can hide people who saved nothing or lost time fixing the output.
  • No Error Bars, No Result likely A percentage from a small group or a single trial needs an interval and a count.
  • Distribution Shift worth checking Pilot users and early adopters may not look like the people you would roll it out to.

Safety or risk claim

  • Presence, Not Absence strong match A failed attempt to find a risk shows how hard someone tried; it does not show the risk is absent.
  • The Construct Gap strong match “Safe” can cover more than any single test measures. Ask what was measured, in what setting, and what a passing result would not show.
  • The Functionality Fallacy likely A safety claim is a performance claim: ask who checked it independently, on what, and how often it fails.
  • Evaluation Awareness likely A model that can tell it is being tested may behave differently from how it will in real use.
  • Prompt Sensitivity likely Safety behavior can change with phrasing, so one set of prompts does not cover how people will actually ask.
  • Once Is Not Reliable worth checking Holding up once is not holding up every time. Ask for repeated-run rates.
  • The Averaging Trap worth checking One safety rate can hide categories or user groups where it fails.

Demo or anecdote

  • The Lab-to-Field Gap strong match A demo is not evidence from real use.
  • Once Is Not Reliable strong match A demo shows it can work once, not how often it does.
  • The Functionality Fallacy likely A polished example does not show the system works on your data.
  • The Jagged Frontier likely Capability is uneven. A demo shows a strength; ask where the failure pockets are.
  • The Reproducibility Rule likely A demo the reader cannot rerun is an anecdote until the setup is recorded.

Agent or task success rate

  • Once Is Not Reliable strong match Agent runs can vary. Ask how many attempts per task and how often it succeeds on every one.
  • No Error Bars, No Result likely A success rate over a small task set needs an interval.
  • The Cost Frontier likely Agents can buy accuracy with retries and tokens. Ask for cost and time per task.
  • The Lab-to-Field Gap likely Real tasks bring messy tools, permissions, and users that a test harness leaves out.
  • The Baseline Rule likely Compare with a simple scripted pipeline or a person doing the same task.
  • The Reproducibility Rule worth checking Agent results depend on the scaffold, tools, and model version. Check that all are recorded.

Human-level or general ability

  • The Construct Gap strong match “Human-level” and “reasoning” are claims about a capability. Ask what the test actually measured.
  • The Baseline Rule strong match Which humans? Experts or crowd workers, with what time and tools, is part of the result.
  • The Jagged Frontier likely Matching people on hard tasks does not mean matching them on easy ones.
  • Data Contamination likely Exams and puzzles often appear online, so the model may have seen them.
  • Benchmark Saturation likely A top score on an older test may only mean the test is used up.
  • The Clever Hans Effect likely A model can pass for the wrong reasons, such as patterns in the questions.
  • The Gold Standard Myth worth checking The human answers are labels too, and they carry error.

High-stakes setting (health, law, hiring, credit)

  • The Functionality Fallacy strong match In a high-stakes setting, ask first for independent evidence that the system works on your kind of cases.
  • Distribution Shift strong match Performance at one hospital, court, or employer may not carry over to another population.
  • The Lab-to-Field Gap strong match Retrospective accuracy is not the same as what changes when people use it in practice.
  • The Team Is the System likely In practice a person reviews or acts on the output. Test the person and the AI together.
  • The Averaging Trap likely One accuracy figure can hide poor performance for particular patients, applicants, or groups.
  • The Baseline Rule strong match Compare with the current standard of practice, not with nothing.
  • The Gold Standard Myth likely Reference labels such as diagnoses or past decisions are judgments with error.
  • Data Contamination worth checking Exam questions and public case sets may have been in the training data.

Fairness or bias claim

  • The Averaging Trap strong match An overall average cannot show which groups the system fails.
  • Presence, Not Absence strong match Not finding bias in one test does not show that it is absent.
  • The Construct Gap likely “Fair” can be defined several ways that conflict. Ask which one was measured.
  • Distribution Shift likely Results for the groups in the test data may not hold for the people it is used on.
  • The Gold Standard Myth worth checking Labels and ratings can carry the same bias the test is looking for.
  • No Error Bars, No Result worth checking Small groups give wide intervals. Ask how many examples each group had.
