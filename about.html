About this guide

An independent reference for people who build, buy, or design with AI. It collects the reliable ways AI evaluation goes wrong, with the research behind each one.

What you can rely on

Every claim on a pattern page is traceable to a source listed on that page, and preprints, reports, working papers, and books are labeled as such. On 19 October 2026 I re-audited every claim on the pattern, guide, glossary, and bibliography pages against its primary source where it could be read, and against abstracts or publisher records where it could not, with AI-assisted checking; the methodology says what that covered and what it could not. This guide is independent, with no vendor funding. It is self-published, has not been through formal peer review, and no outside reviewer has signed off on any pattern. If you find an error, tell me through the contact page or a pattern page’s “Report it” link; every correction is recorded in the Changelog.

  • Role tags (Building, Buying, and Designing), the In plain terms summaries, and the questions to ask on each pattern page.
  • The matching rules in the claim checker and the situation finder. Both are published on their own pages.
  • The checklist’s phases and stakes levels: 3 phases and 3 levels of stakes, and where each pattern’s question sits.
  • The models in the guide chapters, each marked in the text: the 5-stage maturity path, the 3 risk tiers and the minimum evidence for each, and the sequences in the Playbook.
  • Rules of thumb, marked as such where they appear: for prompt variants, several, starting with at least three to five; for runs per item, more than one, at least three for stochastic outputs; for human labels to check a judge, a few dozen as a first screen, more when differences are small; and a few dozen outputs to read. When a sentence in the guide chapters is this guide’s own, it says so in the text. See “How to read the labels” at the top of the Playbook.

What a “pattern” means here

A pattern here is a reliable way evaluation goes wrong, with research behind it. It is not a law of physics. Some are established terms, like Goodhart’s Law. Others are named here: the idea is well documented, and the name is this guide’s own, chosen to make it easier to remember. Each page says which.

Editorial policy

  • Every pattern links to its sources. Preprints, reports, regulations, working papers, and books are labeled as such. Every other source appeared in a peer-reviewed venue or is a long-established classic, apart from a few commentary pieces and older documents that the Bibliography tags. The methodology explains how sources are counted and how the evidence was checked.
  • Each pattern shows when its evidence was last checked and carries a version number and revision history.
  • The Changelog records what changed and when. Corrections are made in the open.
  • Role tags, plain-terms summaries, checklist questions, and claim-checker rules are editorial work built from the patterns themselves. They are starting points, not authority (see what is this guide’s own).

How strong is the evidence?

Each pattern page carries an Evidence base label. The label describes how many independent, mostly peer-reviewed sources the page cites. It does not say how large or how certain the effect is, it does not rate the whole research literature on the topic, and it does not say a pattern is more or less important. What the studies found, including where they found little or no effect, is in the page’s Evidence section. The label is computed from the source list by fixed rules, so anyone can check it.

  • Well sourced (16): at least 4 sources, at least 4 of them peer-reviewed or classic works making up at least 60% of the list, and those works have at least 3 different lead authors. The page cites several independent studies.
  • Developing (7): at least 3 sources, at least 2 of them peer-reviewed or classic, but short of the bar above. The list is smaller, or leans on preprints and reports.
  • Thin (3): everything else. Typically one peer-reviewed source or fewer, with the rest preprints or reports. The idea may still be sound; the page cites few sources for it so far.

“Peer-reviewed or classic” means any source not labeled a preprint, report, working paper, or book on the pattern page. “Lead author” is the first author listed, used as a rough stand-in for a separate research group. The rules count sources. They cannot tell whether a study is good, and a pattern can rest on a few strong papers or a large pile of weak ones. A well-sourced page can also report a small or contested effect: Adaptive Overfitting is well sourced, and its Evidence section reports that the large studies found little or no sign of the effect. Read the Evidence section and the sources themselves. The same limits apply to the labels: they are an editorial summary, not a measurement. The rules were written for this guide and are not a published standard.

Who maintains it

Hi, I’m Joseph Alfonso, a UX design lead. I made this guide because I wanted to understand how AI really gets judged, and how those judgments go wrong. Writing it down was how I learned. I chose the patterns, built the guide and its tools, and maintain it.

The evidence has been checked twice: a spot-check of specific figures in early October 2026, then a full re-audit of every claim against its primary source on 19 October 2026. The re-audit was my own, done with AI-assisted checking, not by an outside expert. The methodology says exactly what the checks covered and where a source could not be read in full. Where the evidence is mixed or limited, the page says so.

I care about this because AI can feel like something that happens to us. I don’t think it has to. The more we understand how it works and why it fails, the more we can use it on our own terms and ask better questions of the people selling it, building it, or telling us to trust it. I’m still learning, and I’ve tried to be honest about what I know and don’t.

No outside reviewer has signed off on any pattern yet. If you work in evaluation research or statistics and would review a pattern, or you find a mistake or a better source, please tell me through the contact page. I’d be grateful, and I’ll fix it. I hope this helps you the way making it helped me.
