Criteria Drift

“You often figure out what ‘good’ means by grading outputs, so expect your criteria to change.”

The bottom line

Version your grading criteria, and re-grade a fixed reference set whenever they change.

Keep in mind: Most useful while you are still learning what good looks like; this is a sensible practice the study did not test.

In plain terms

You work out what “good” means by reading real outputs, so your rubric is likely to change as you grade. Version it, and re-grade a fixed set when it does.

Takeaways #

  • In the study that named this pattern, practitioners could not fully specify their criteria before grading real outputs.
  • In that study, grading surfaced new criteria and changed how participants read the ones they had.
  • The study did not test fixes, but a sensible practice is to treat evaluation criteria as a living design artifact, versioned like code.
  • When criteria change, re-grade a fixed reference set or your trend lines stop being comparable.

What it means #

If you’ve done qualitative research, this will feel familiar. You don’t know the themes until you’ve read the transcripts. Evaluation works the same way. Teams sit down to define “a good summary,” write a few criteria, then start reading outputs and realize they care about things they never wrote down, like tone, or whether the summary admits uncertainty. The rubric grows. Some criteria turn out to matter only for certain kinds of outputs.

That’s healthy. The risk is pretending the criteria were fixed all along and comparing numbers graded under different rubrics.

The evidence #

reviewed 19 Oct 2026

Named in a user study. In a qualitative study of nine industry practitioners building LLM-based evaluators with their EvalGen tool, Shankar et al. (2024) described criteria drift: “users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria.” Some criteria depended on the specific outputs people had seen. The study was small and started from one task, extracting entities from tweets.

Start from real needs. Liao and Xiao (2023), in a position paper rather than a study of how rubrics change, argue that evaluation should be grounded in the real-world needs of the people using a system, and that methods from human-computer interaction can help close the gap between what gets measured and what matters.

Use it #

  1. Start with open coding. Read a few dozen real outputs (this guide’s rule of thumb, not a number from the study) and note what’s good and bad before writing any rubric.
  2. Version the rubric, with a date and a reason for each change.
  3. Keep a fixed reference set and re-grade it when the rubric changes. The study did not test this, but it follows from wanting comparable trend lines.
  4. Involve the people who will actually use the output in defining the criteria.

Where this doesn’t apply #

This pattern rests on fewer studies than most. The term and its main evidence come from one qualitative study of nine practitioners by Shankar et al., plus related work. It matters most while you are still working out what good looks like. With settled criteria it matters less.

Origins #

The term comes from Shreya Shankar, J.D. Zamfirescu-Pereira, Björn Hartmann, Aditya Parameswaran, and Ian Arawjo’s 2024 UIST paper, “Who Validates the Validators?” It was coined there. Later papers have used it, but it is a recent term, not a long-established one.
