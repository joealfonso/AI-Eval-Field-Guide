Presence, Not Absence

“An evaluation can show a capability or risk exists. It can’t prove one doesn’t.”

The bottom line

Read a negative result as “not found with this effort,” and ask how hard anyone tried.

Keep in mind: A clean result is still informative; it is weaker than showing nothing is there.

In plain terms

An evaluation can show that a capability or a risk exists. It can’t show that none does, only that it wasn’t found with this much effort.

Takeaways #

  • A failed attempt to get a model to do something tells you about the attempt, not the model’s ceiling.
  • Better prompts, tools, fine-tuning, or more time can unlock abilities an eval missed.
  • Safety claims built on “we tried and it didn’t” need to say how hard someone tried.
  • Report elicitation effort alongside every negative result.

What it means #

Edsger Dijkstra (1970) made this point about software in his Notes on Structured Programming: “Program testing can be used to show the presence of bugs, but never to show their absence!” General-purpose AI systems make it sharper. The space of possible inputs is effectively infinite, and capabilities often show up only with the right prompt, tool, or scaffolding. A clean eval report means you didn’t find the problem under the conditions you tried.

The evidence #

reviewed 19 Oct 2026

Evaluations for extreme risks. Shevlane et al. (2023) describe dangerous capability evaluations and alignment evaluations as key inputs to training and deployment decisions, and discuss the limits of what those evaluations can catch.

Models can be made to underperform. van der Weij et al. (2025) showed that when instructed in the prompt, frontier models like GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus could selectively underperform on dangerous-capability multiple-choice tests while keeping performance on general ones. They also fine-tuned models to hide a capability unless given a password. The authors tested whether models can do this, not whether they do it on their own.

Red teaming turns up a wide range of harms. Ganguli et al. (2022) released a dataset of nearly 39,000 red team attacks on language models. The harmful outputs ranged from offensive language to subtler unethical content.

Use it #

  1. Phrase negative results precisely: “Under these conditions, with this effort, we didn’t observe X.”
  2. Report what elicitation was tried: prompts, tools, fine-tuning, number of attempts, and time spent.
  3. Budget for red teaming by people with real domain expertise.
  4. Revisit negative results when models, tools, or techniques change.

Where this doesn’t apply #

A clean result is still informative. The pattern is about wording what it means: the tests found nothing, which is weaker than showing nothing is there. The sandbagging evidence tests whether models can be made to hide a capability, not whether they do so on their own.

Origins #

The core idea is Dijkstra’s, from “Notes on Structured Programming,” first circulated in 1969 with a second edition in 1970. “Presence, Not Absence” is this site’s name for applying it to AI evaluation.
