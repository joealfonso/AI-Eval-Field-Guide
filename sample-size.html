Sample size and error bars

Every score on a test set is an estimate. How far it could move if you had drawn different items depends mostly on how many items you used. This page shows how to put numbers on that, and how many items you need before a comparison means anything.

The short version

  • A score on 200 items is a range, not a point. For 200 items scored at 80%, the Wilson 95% interval runs from 73.9% to 85.0%.
  • Halving the margin of error takes about four times as many items. Going from ±10 points to ±5 takes you from roughly 100 to roughly 385 items; ±3 takes about 1,070.
  • Telling two systems apart needs far more items than measuring one. Detecting 80% against 85% on two separate samples takes about 906 items per system. On the same items, if the systems disagree on 10% of them, about 312.
  • If two systems differ by less than the noise, nobody has shown they differ. See No Error Bars, No Result.
  • A single run of a single prompt is also a sample. See Once Is Not Reliable and Prompt Sensitivity.

Margins are in percentage points, using the usual 95% formula 1.96 × √(p(1−p)/n). Accuracy near 50% is the widest case, so the first column is the worst case. Assumes independent items scored right or wrong.

Worked examples

  • Is 82% vs 79% a win? On 200 items, a score of 80% has a 95% interval of 73.9% to 85.0%, roughly five to six points either side. A 3-point gap sits well inside that. Even when both systems run on the same items, a 3-point gap on 200 items is distinguishable from noise only if the systems disagree on fewer than about 5% of items.
  • Same items, small disagreement. To detect a 5-point gap with 80% power when the systems disagree on 10% of items you need about 312 items. If they disagree on 20% of items, about 626. More disagreement means more noise in the comparison, so you need more items.
  • Different items. The same 5-point gap on two separate samples needs about 906 items per system, because nothing is cancelled out by sharing items.
  • Do not know the disagreement rate? Run both systems on 50 to 100 items first and count the items where exactly one is right.

Every figure above and in the table is checked by build/test_sample_size.py in the site repository, which also confirms the power by exact calculation rather than relying on the approximation.

What the numbers assume, and where they fail

  • Independent items. The formulas treat each item as an independent draw. Items that come in groups (several questions about one document, or many prompts from one user) are correlated, and the true interval is wider. Miller (2024) gives a clustered standard error for this. With strong clustering, count the groups, not the items.
  • Items are a sample from what you care about. The interval only covers the luck of which items you drew. It says nothing about whether the test items resemble real use. See Distribution Shift and The Construct Gap.
  • Judge noise is extra. If an AI model grades the answers, its errors and preferences add to the uncertainty and can bias the score in one direction. The interval above does not include that. Check the grader against people (Judge Bias).
  • Model randomness is separate. Running each item several times reduces noise from the model’s own variation, but not noise from which items you picked. Both matter (Once Is Not Reliable).
  • Approximations. Wilson intervals behave well at small n and for accuracy near 0% or 100%; the planning formulas for sample size do not. Below about 30 items, or with fewer than about 25 disagreements in a paired comparison, treat the figures as rough and consider an exact method.
  • Many comparisons. The calculators cover one comparison. If you test many variants against one test set and report the best, the best will look better than it is (Adaptive Overfitting). This page does not cover corrections for multiple comparisons.
  • Point of the plan. The sample size depends on the smallest difference that would change your decision. Decide that first and write it down (see the eval plan template).

The formulas in the calculators are standard textbook results; this page applies them to eval data and does not add to them. The sample-size formulas in 3a and 3b are normal approximations. Miller’s worked example for detecting a 3-point difference, under the assumptions in his paper, comes to roughly 1,000 questions.
