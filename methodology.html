Methodology

How the patterns were chosen, what counts as a source, how the evidence was checked, and what this guide does not claim to be.

How patterns are chosen

The 26 patterns are an editorial selection of recurring ways AI evaluation goes wrong, each with published research behind it. They are not the result of a systematic review, and another reasonable list would differ. Each pattern page says whether its name is an established term, a recent term, or named here (the idea is established, but the name is this guide’s own), and gives an Evidence base of well established, developing, or emerging (see how the evidence base is described).

For what comes from the research and what is this guide’s own, see the line drawn on the About page. The table of every pattern with its name label and Evidence base is in the same page.

What counts as a source

A citation is one source line on one pattern page, so a work cited by three patterns is three citations. A work is one distinct piece of research, counted once however many pages cite it. Two versions of one study count as one work: the 2023 working paper and the 2026 published version of Dell’Acqua et al.

The 26 pattern pages make 120 citations to 96 distinct works. The Bibliography lists 115 works in all, which adds 19 that appear only in the Overview, Being Pragmatic, Playbook or Glossary. Counting each work once, of the 115, 95 appeared in a peer-reviewed venue or are long-established classics, 12 are preprints, 2 are reports, 1 is a regulation, and 5 are books. A few unlabeled entries are not ordinary peer-reviewed papers, and the Bibliography tags them: commentary pieces (Burnell et al., 2023, a Science Policy Forum article; Lin, 2019, an opinion paper in ACM SIGIR Forum; Lipton and Steinhardt, 2019, in the practitioner magazine ACM Queue); older classics that were not journal articles (Dijkstra, 1970, a technical note; Goodhart, 1975, a conference paper).

Preprints, reports, regulations, working papers, and books are labeled on the pattern page and in the Bibliography. The Evidence section reports what the authors found, and where results are mixed or limited the page says so, for example in Adaptive Overfitting and Data Contamination. Every pattern also has a “Where this doesn’t apply” section.

How the evidence was checked

Each pattern shows when its evidence was last reviewed (currently 19 Oct 2026). The evidence has been checked twice, and neither check is independent peer review.

  • Early October 2026: a spot-check. More than 40 specific figures and findings on the pattern pages were compared with the source’s abstract or full text, covering at least one claim in 25 of the 26 patterns. It found no discrepancies, but it compared only numbers and headline findings, so it could not catch statements that went beyond their sources or left out a caveat.
  • 19 October 2026: a full re-audit. Every claim on the pattern pages, the guide pages, the glossary, and the bibliography was checked against its primary source, including whether each statement was supported as strongly as it was worded. Every DOI was checked against Crossref and every arXiv entry against arXiv’s records. This found five factual errors (a misquoted sentence, a wrong author name, a wrong statistical rule, two experiments reported as one, and a measure described as something it was not), plus several dozen statements that were stronger than their sources or missing a caveat. Those have been corrected, softened, or removed. Every source link was also tested: none returned a not-found error, although some publishers block automated checks. 5 sources are listed without a link.

Limits of the re-audit. Some sources could not be read in full: paywalled journal articles, some conference papers in the ACM Digital Library, books, and recent legal texts were checked against abstracts, publisher records, or secondary descriptions, and the pages are worded to match what could be verified. Whether a preprint has since been published in a journal was not checked exhaustively. The re-audit was carried out by the site’s author with AI-assisted checking, not by an outside expert, so errors may remain. If you find one, please report it.

The tools

The claim checker, Find your patterns, the checklist, the quick brief, and Use it now are reading aids. The claim checker and Find your patterns match your text against published rules, which you can read on each page. They do not assess a system, and they run in your browser. The Design Rubric and Readiness Review are checklists. They do not calculate a score. The sample-size calculators apply standard formulas, listed on that page and checked by a test script in the site repository. The case studies summarize published studies and link to each primary source. The templates are editorial starting points.

What this guide does not cover

These are gaps, not oversights. Each is a field of its own, and the guide treats it only briefly or not at all.

  • Statistics beyond the basics. The sample-size page covers confidence intervals and the number of items for one or two comparisons. It does not cover statistical power in complex designs, corrections for many comparisons, Bayesian methods, or items that come in correlated groups beyond a brief mention.
  • Calibration. Whether a system’s stated confidence matches how often it is right is not covered.
  • Agent and tool-use evaluation. Agents appear through a few benchmarks and studies, such as τ-bench. Designing evaluations for multi-step agents, tool use, and long-running tasks is not covered in depth.
  • Safety and red-team methods. Presence, Not Absence explains why a failure to find a risk is weak evidence. How to run a red-team exercise, design adversarial tests, or assess dangerous capabilities is not covered.
  • Validating an AI grader. Judge Bias says to check a grader against people on a sample. It does not cover agreement measures, rubric design for judges, or how many labels are enough.
  • Building evaluation sets at scale. Sampling, annotation pipelines, quality control, and keeping a set fresh over time are not covered.
  • Fairness auditing, privacy, security, and legal compliance. These are touched on by the Readiness Review, which points to standards, but they are not treated as evaluation methods here.
  • Evidence from the last few months. Each pattern shows when its evidence was last checked. Newer work may not be reflected yet.

If you know a good source for any of these, please say so through the contact page.

What this guide is not

  • It is not a ranking or benchmark of any AI model or vendor.
  • It is not legal, regulatory, or compliance advice, though the Readiness Review points to standards that are relevant.
  • It is not peer reviewed, and no outside reviewer has signed off on any pattern yet.

How it changes

Each pattern has a version number and a revision history, and site-wide changes are in the changelog. Corrections are made in the open and logged there. If you find a mistake or a better source, every pattern page has a “Report it” link, or you can use the contact page.
